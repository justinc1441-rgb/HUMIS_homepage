Pre-Columbian and colonial Mexico established a deeply structured society where women managed households and family life, yet faced severe legal and social restrictions. In indigenous cultures like the Aztec and Maya, women held vital economic roles in local markets and weaving, though they generally could not own land. Following the Spanish conquest in the 16th century, society reorganized around the casta system and strict Catholic ideals like marianismo, which emphasized female submission and purity, while figures such as the interpreter Malinche and the intellectual nun Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz left lasting yet complex marks on Mexican history.

The Mexican Revolution of 1910 acted as a major turning point, pulling women out of traditional domestic confines as they served as soldaderas (soldiers, nurses, and suppliers), spies, and political organizers. This period sparked early feminist milestones, including the First Feminist Congress in Mérida in 1916 and progressive legal reforms for divorce and alimony. Decades of grassroots activism eventually culminated in constitutional reform on October 17, 1953, which officially granted Mexican women full national suffrage and the right to hold public office.

In contemporary decades, women have expanded their presence across the workforce, education, and the highest levels of government. After achieving legislative gender parity in Congress and seeing milestones like Norma Piña become the first female chief justice of the Supreme Court, Mexico reached its ultimate political milestone in 2024 with the election of Claudia Sheinbaum as the country's first female president. Despite these monumental political and cultural achievements, women across Mexico continue to fight systemic challenges, including high rates of femicide, domestic violence, and persistent economic inequality
